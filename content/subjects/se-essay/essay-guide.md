# SE Aptitude Test essay — guide, structure and rubric

## What CMU says

Part 2 of the Software Engineering Aptitude Test is "demonstration by writing/essay" — one question. CMU does not publish the prompt, word limit or rubric, so prepare for the common prompt types below and practise writing under time pressure (about 40–45 minutes). The essay is written in English, so it also shows your written English.

## Likely prompt types

1. **Motivation:** "Why do you want to study software engineering at CMU?" / "Describe a moment that made you interested in technology."
2. **Technology and society:** "How will artificial intelligence change the job of a software engineer?" / "Discuss one benefit and one risk of social media apps."
3. **Problem solving / design:** "Describe an app or system you would build to solve a problem in your community." / "Explain how you would design a system for a library to lend books."
4. **Experience:** "Describe a project you built or a technical problem you solved. What did you learn?"
5. **Ethics:** "Should software companies be responsible for how users use their products?" / "Discuss data privacy."
6. **Teamwork / future goals:** "Where do you see yourself after graduation?" / "Describe working in a team."

## Recommended structure (≈ 350–500 words)

1. **Introduction (2–4 sentences):** hook, context, and a clear thesis that answers the question directly.
2. **Body paragraph 1:** first main point + specific example (a project, an app you use, a real event).
3. **Body paragraph 2:** second main point + example. For design prompts: users, key features, how it works (inputs, processing, outputs, data), and challenges.
4. **Body paragraph 3 (optional):** a counter-argument or limitation and your response — shows critical thinking.
5. **Conclusion (2–3 sentences):** restate the thesis in new words and link to your future in software engineering.

## Timing plan for 45 minutes

- 5 minutes: read the prompt, brainstorm, outline (thesis + 2–3 points + examples).
- 30 minutes: write.
- 5–10 minutes: proofread grammar, spelling, and that every paragraph supports the thesis.

## What graders typically look for (practice rubric, 20 points)

| Criterion | Points | Strong essay |
|---|---|---|
| Task response | 5 | Answers exactly what was asked; clear thesis; stays on topic |
| Ideas and reasoning | 5 | Logical arguments, specific examples, shows understanding of technology and SE |
| Organisation | 4 | Intro–body–conclusion; one idea per paragraph; transitions (firstly, however, as a result) |
| Language | 4 | Correct grammar and spelling; varied sentences; appropriate vocabulary |
| Insight / originality | 2 | Personal perspective, awareness of trade-offs, ethics or users |

This rubric is a study tool modelled on common university essay criteria, not CMU's official rubric.

## Useful vocabulary and phrases

- Technology: user experience, scalability, reliability, automation, data privacy, algorithm, open source, digital transformation.
- Linking: furthermore, in addition, on the other hand, nevertheless, consequently, for instance, in conclusion.
- Hedging and opinion: "I believe…", "This suggests that…", "One possible drawback is…".

## Common mistakes

- Writing a list of features without explaining why they matter.
- No examples — vague claims like "technology is very important".
- Memorised essays that don't answer the actual prompt.
- Running out of time before the conclusion. Always leave a conclusion, even if short.
- Overly complex vocabulary used incorrectly — clarity beats complexity.

## Mini example (design prompt)

Prompt: "Describe an app you would build to help your community."

Thesis: "I would build a bilingual mobile app that connects small local farmers directly with nearby buyers, because it would raise farmers' income and reduce food waste."
Body 1: the problem (middlemen take most profit; unsold produce rots) with a concrete local example.
Body 2: how it works — farmers post produce with photos and prices; buyers order and pay by QR code; a map shows pickup points; notifications for fresh listings.
Body 3: challenges — farmers with low digital literacy → a simple interface, voice input, and training at the village office.
Conclusion: software engineering lets me turn this kind of idea into a reliable product, which is why I want to study it at CMU.
